When the world moves,
a good detector starts lying

A fault detector was fitted on a healthy distribution grid. Then the operating regime changed: more load, warmer weather. Its detection rate stayed at 100% and its detection delay fell to zero. It also became almost entirely useless. This page simulates two energy systems, reproduces that result, and tests what actually fixes it.

Bolgaç Gülen · Odense · built August 2026 for the PhD position in Energy Informatics, Theme 2, predictive maintenance and asset management of smart energy networks. Everything below runs in this page: no server, no libraries.

Under a regime change, the baseline detector reported 100% detection with zero delay while 99% of the tickets it raised were on healthy assets. Uncertainty gating cut the nuisance load fivefold but pushed median detection out to 80 days. Only recalibration recovered precision, and it cost more than half the detections. There is no configuration here that is simply better. That is the finding.

The second system, where it does not work

The same stack, unchanged, on a fleet of district heating substations losing heat-exchanger effectiveness. It goes considerably worse, and the failure is more interesting than the success.

Precision never recovers. Two reasons, and neither is a tuning problem.

First, the heating fleet is seasonally non-stationary by construction. Return temperature depends on weather through both demand and the weather-compensated supply curve, so a model fitted on ninety winter days is extrapolating by spring. The residual drifts for reasons that have nothing to do with any fault, and no threshold placed on that residual can tell the difference.

Second, and worse, the drift monitor does not fire on this system. The regime change moves supply temperature and demand, but both stay inside the range the ensemble was fitted over, so the models continue to agree with each other while being jointly wrong. Ensemble disagreement detects novelty, not error, and those come apart exactly when the change is a shift along a direction the training data already covered. On this fleet the recalibration arm therefore never triggers, which is why its row is barely distinguishable from the plain gate.

The honest conclusion is that a monitoring architecture is not portable between two asset classes inside the same utility, even when the detector, the gates and the calibration procedure are identical. What transfers is the method; what does not transfer is the assumption that the healthy window represents the operating envelope. Deciding that in advance, per asset class, looks to me like the actual engineering problem, and it is the part I would want to work on.

What the gate actually does

The stack mirrors how this centre's own published pipelines are put together, at a scale that fits in a browser.

  • An ensemble learns healthy behaviour. Eight bootstrapped quadratic ridge models predict the observable, transformer top-oil temperature rise or substation return temperature, from load and weather. Their mean is the expectation; their disagreement is the epistemic uncertainty. This stands in for MC dropout over several architectures, which is the heavier version used in the literature below.
  • Three statistics watch the residual. A standardised residual, an EWMA, and a one-sided CUSUM. Thresholds are not hand-tuned: each is set from the healthy window so that all three fire at the same small rate, which is what makes the two systems comparable.
  • Gate 1, vote. At least two of the three statistics must agree.
  • Gate 2, uncertainty. The alarm is suppressed while ensemble disagreement sits in the top 2% of what was seen during training. The detector abstains rather than guesses.
  • Gate 3, persistence. The vote must hold for six consecutive hours.
  • Recalibration. A separate drift monitor watches fleet-average disagreement. When it stays elevated for three days, the world is declared to have moved: the ensemble is refit on a fresh healthy window and the statistics reset. The detector is deliberately blind while this happens, and that blindness is counted against it in the table above.

What I take from it

Three things, none of them comfortable.

Detection rate is the wrong headline number. In the shifted world the naive detector scores perfectly on detection and delay, because it is alarming on everything. Precision is the column that exposes it, and precision is the column an operator feels. A monitoring system evaluated on recall alone will be optimised straight into being ignored.

Abstention buys honesty, not performance. Gating on uncertainty does exactly what it should: the model stops asserting things it cannot support. But on its own it is a delay machine, pushing median detection from days to weeks, which for a fault that develops over a month means catching it after it has arrived. Abstention is a way to stay truthful while something else fixes the problem, not the fix itself.

The fix is a lifecycle action, not a better statistic. Precision only recovers when the system notices the regime changed and refits. That is an operational capability, not a model property, and it is the part that a paper about detection accuracy will never measure.

Where this came from

The design of the stack, and the metrics used to score it, are taken from the SDU Center for Energy Informatics and from the literature it builds on. I have marked the centre's own work.

The methods

  • Søndergaard, H.A.N., Shaker, H.R., Jørgensen, B.N. (2026). Multi-method fault detection considering uncertainty through MC dropout for enhanced voting. Energy and Buildings, 356, 117082. centre
    The source of the shape of this pipeline: several models, uncertainty carried through inference, and a vote that weights each contribution by its inverse variance rather than treating all votes as equal. My three-statistic vote with an uncertainty veto is the small version of it.
  • Ma, Z.G., Cong, L., Jørgensen, B.N. (2026). Deployment feasibility as a layered construct: a sequential gate framework for evaluating battery dispatch strategies in distribution grids. Energies, 19(10), 2424. centre
    The reason this page compares architectures rather than thresholds. That paper shows that whether criteria are applied flat or as a sequence of non-compensable gates changes the recommendation on identical data. This page asks the same question of alarm logic.
  • Shadi, M.R., Mirshekali, H., Tahavori, M., Shaker, H.R. (2026). Survival models for predictive maintenance and remaining useful life in sensor-enabled smart energy networks: a review. Sensors, 26(6), 1915. centre
    Where the fault model comes from: degradation as a hazard that rises with age and stress, and the warning that a young fleet may never observe its own wear-out phase.
  • Shadi, M.R., Mirshekali, H., Shaker, H.R. (2025). Explainable artificial intelligence for energy systems maintenance. Renewable and Sustainable Energy Reviews, 216, 115668. centre
    The argument that maintenance AI has to be scored on what it costs an operator to act on, not on classification accuracy. The ticket-and-precision framing here follows from it.
  • Mirshekali, H., Ghanadi Ladani, F., Shaker, H.R. (2025). Consequence-aware prescriptive maintenance with Transformer-KAN forecasting and PPO-controlled grid reconfiguration. IEEE Transactions on Smart Grid, 16(5), 4237–4251. centre
    Alarms weighted by how many consumers sit behind them. Not implemented here, and the obvious next thing to add: my tickets are currently all worth the same, which is the least realistic assumption on this page.
  • Værbak, M., Billanes, J.D., Jørgensen, B.N., Ma, Z.G. (2024). A digital twin framework for simulating distributed energy resources in distribution grids. Energies, 17(11), 2503. centre
    Where the simulated fleet gets its shape: real Danish residential feeders, a 400 kVA transformer per district, and the finding that electric vehicle charging, not solar, is what eventually overloads them.

The literature underneath it

  • Gal, Y., Ghahramani, Z. (2016). Dropout as a Bayesian approximation: representing model uncertainty in deep learning. arXiv:1506.02142. The origin of treating disagreement as epistemic uncertainty. A bootstrapped ensemble is the cruder, cheaper cousin used here.
  • Page, E.S. (1954). Continuous inspection schemes. Biometrika, 41, 100–115. The CUSUM statistic, one of the three voters.
  • Roberts, S.W. (1959). Control chart tests based on geometric moving averages. Technometrics, 1, 239–250. The EWMA, the second voter.
  • Kaplan, E.L., Meier, P. (1958). Nonparametric estimation from incomplete observations. Journal of the American Statistical Association, 53, 457–481. Censoring, and why a fleet that has not failed yet still carries information.
  • Lundberg, S.M., Lee, S.-I. (2017). A unified approach to interpreting model predictions. NeurIPS 30. The attribution machinery the centre's explainability review evaluates.
  • Endsley, M.R. (2017). From here to autonomy: lessons learned from human-automation research. Human Factors, 59, 5–27. Why an alarm that a human cannot act on is not a detection.

What this is not

A simulation, and a small one. The systems are plausible rather than validated: a first-order thermal lag for transformers, an effectiveness decay for heat exchangers, no network topology, no real measurements, no consumer weighting on alarms, and faults that arrive one at a time. Every number above is generated by the code on this page, so none of it is evidence about a real grid. It is evidence about how these detector architectures behave relative to each other when the ground moves, which is the only claim I am making.
